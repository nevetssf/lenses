# Leica Elmar 50mm f/3.5 (Collapsible)

The Elmar 50mm f/3.5 is the lens that defined the Leica system. First offered in 1925 on the Leica I, it was produced in various forms through 1961 and remains one of the most recognizable rangefinder lenses ever made. The optical formula — four elements in three groups (Tessar type) — was designed by Max Berek and delivers sharp, contrasty results that punch well above its modest maximum aperture.

The lens is collapsible: the barrel retracts into the camera body for compact storage, then locks out for use. Early examples are nickel-plated ("Nickel Elmar," 1925–1932), later replaced by chrome-plated versions from the mid-1930s onward. A "red scale" variant, marked with red aperture and distance numbers, was produced from around 1951. Uncoated until 1946; coated ("red T" or simply coated) thereafter.

Common service issues are stiff or seized collapsing action from dried grease, oil on the aperture blades, and haze on internal surfaces. The aperture ring on early versions sits at the front of the lens and turns by pinching two small tabs — an unusual arrangement that surprises first-time users.
