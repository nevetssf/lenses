My copy arrived with serious internal haze and traces of oil on the blades. This is a common occurrence with vintage lense from the 1950's and 60's. The oil they used tended to evaporate and leave deposits on the lens surfaces closest to the blades, and migrate around, showing up most clearly on the blades. In the best case the haze just wipes off with a decent cleaner like ROR or Eclipse, but sometimes the oil can polymerize or etch the glass and leave damage that can only be removed by re-polishing. I didn't know which I was dealing with before opening up the lens. 

Fortunately, there are some good resources online, and the lense is relatively straightforward to service.

## Tools & Materials

- Spanner wrench (adjustable)
- Heavy padded rubber gloves
- Set of black rubber cones
- 99.9% isopropyl alcohol
- Acetone
- Cotton swabs (q-tips)
- Kimwipes
- Testors Enamel Model Paint — Gloss Dark Bright Red and Gloss Black
- Toothpick (for stirring paint)

---



## Removing the optical block

The optical block unscrews from the main body. It can be quite tight, but if you use good rubber gloves and firm pressure it will come apart. 

You can see the haze in the optical elements in this photo. 

![Optical block separated from the helicoid assembly](images/summilux-50mm-f1.4-pre-asph-v1/IMG_7669.jpeg)

This optical block consists of the front elements, the aperture, and the rear elements. The front portion uncrews from the main optical block. It can be very tight, and in my case it was shelacked at one point, I assume to keep it from rotating. I managed to get it loose by squirting a bit of 99.9% ISO into the threads, a thing which I don't really like doing because it tends to go everywhere and would spread any alcohol-soluble inks all over the place, but it ended up working out fine.

## Re-painting the lettering

The black and red distance scales were missing a lot of paint, so I cleaned them with acetone using a cotton swab and repainted them using Testors Enamel Model Paint. I didn't go crazy removing all the paint, since there wasn't much left and the new paint should bond to it easily. The procedure is documented elsewhere and everyone seems to have a slightly different approach, so I'll just outline what I did.

- Used Testors Enamel Gloss Dark Bright Red and and Gloss Black for the lettering. There is a regular Gloss Red, but it was a bit too orange. Stir the paint well with a toothpick or other small implement.
- After cleaning the existing lettering with a q-tip, apply a tiny bit of paint to the lettering, just enough to fill in the grooves. Let it sit for minute or two, then use a Kimwipe folded over a few times to wipe off the excess, across the lettering. The first wipe will be a bit of a mess. Don't press too hard or you'll remove the recessed paint. The put a drop of 99.9% Isopropyl on a clean section of the folded kimwipe and do a few passes to get rid of the remaining excess paint. You'll get a feel for how much pressure to apply with practice. A Kimwipe is useful becuase it's thin and doesn't shred or leave lint. 
- Proceed in sets of 2-3 letters or words.

It'll take a few days for the lettering to fully cure, but you can handle it (carefully) right away.
